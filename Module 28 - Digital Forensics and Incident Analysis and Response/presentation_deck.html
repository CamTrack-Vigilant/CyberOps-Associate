28.1 Evidence Handling & Attack Attribution • 28.1.1

Digital Forensics: Evidence & Legal Context

📁

What is Digital Forensics?

Digital forensics is the recovery and investigation of information found on digital devices as it relates to criminal activity.

  • Indicators of Compromise (IoC): Tangible evidence that an incident occurred (disk files, volatile RAM, pcaps, logs).
  • Tier 1 Role: Tier 1 analysts are often the first to uncover wrongdoing and must handle evidence so it supports legal prosecution.
  • Essential Mandate: All IoCs must be preserved in untampered condition for future analysis and threat attribution.
⚖️

Investigation Contexts & Regulations

  • Private (Internal) Investigations: Focus on internal policy violations. If criminal conduct or intellectual property theft occurs, it becomes public by calling law enforcement.
  • Public Investigations: Carried out by public officials when internal users or external attackers violate legal statutes.
  • Regulatory Mandates (e.g., US HIPAA): Requires notification if patient records breach. If >500 individuals in a jurisdiction are impacted, both the media and affected victims must be notified. Forensics certifies the numbers.
  • Organization Under Investigation: The company itself may be investigated. Analysts must preserve evidence; intentional destruction results in criminal penalties.
📖 Course Note: Private vs. Public & HIPAA Rule
Under HIPAA regulations, if a data breach involves patient information, notification must be made. If the breach involves more than 500 individuals in a jurisdiction, the media and victims must be notified. Digital forensics certifies the scope and exact count.

28.1 Evidence Handling • 28.1.2 The Forensic Process

The NIST SP 800-86 Forensic Process

Collection is Critical

Identification of potential sources of forensic data and acquisition, handling, and storage of that data. Special care must be taken not to damage, lose, or omit important data.

NIST SP 800-86 Guidance

Organizations must develop well-documented procedures. In the event of a public investigation, regulatory bodies and courts may inspect these records to certify data handling integrity.

📖 Toggle Exact Course Note Summary
28.1.2 NIST SP 800-86 Forensic Process:
1. Collection (Media): Identify, acquire, label, and securely store media without altering original contents.
2. Examination (Data): Extract relevant digital artifacts and filter routine/benign operating system noise.
3. Analysis (Information): Correlate artifacts to reconstruct the intrusion timeline and draw actionable conclusions.
4. Reporting (Evidence): Document procedures, findings, and forensic conclusions for stakeholders and court presentation.
28.1 Evidence Handling • 28.1.4 Types of Evidence

Classifications of Legal Evidence

👁️

Direct Evidence

Evidence that was indisputably in the possession of the accused, or is eyewitness evidence from someone who directly observed criminal behavior.

  • Requires no presumption or inference.
  • Example: CCTV footage showing an unauthorized person typing on the console.
🧩

Indirect (Circumstantial) Evidence

Evidence that, in combination with other facts, establishes a hypothesis.

  • Example: Evidence that an individual has committed similar crimes supports the assertion they committed this crime.
  • In cybercrime, most TTP correlations are circumstantial.
💎

Best Evidence

Evidence that is in its original state. This could be storage devices used by an accused, or verified archives of files that can be proven to be unaltered (e.g., bit-stream disk images).

🔗

Corroborating Evidence

Evidence that supports an assertion that is developed from best evidence (e.g., corroborating firewall connection logs that match an executable found on the suspect's hard drive).

📖 Toggle Exact Course Note Summary
28.1.4 Legal Evidence Classifications:
• Direct Evidence: Indisputably in possession of accused or direct eyewitness testimony.
• Indirect (Circumstantial) Evidence: Combined facts establishing a logical hypothesis (e.g., prior crimes, matching TTPs).
• Best Evidence: Unaltered original state (e.g., bit-stream forensic disk clone verified by SHA-256 hash).
• Corroborating Evidence: Supplementary evidence supporting best evidence (e.g., secondary server logs confirming endpoint alerts).
28.1 Evidence Handling • 28.1.6 RFC 3227 Volatility

RFC 3227: Order of Volatility

1. Memory registers, caches Most Volatile
2. Routing table, ARP cache, process table, kernel stats, RAM Volatile Memory
3. Temporary file systems Transient FS
4. Non-volatile media, fixed & removable (Disks, USBs) Local Storage
5. Remote logging and monitoring data (Syslog, SIEM) Remote Telemetry
6. Physical interconnections and topologies Physical / Net
7. Archival media, tape or other backups Least Volatile
⚠️

Why Volatility Matters

Data stored in RAM is lost the instant power is cut. Furthermore, routine OS tasks will overwrite unallocated RAM. Therefore, digital evidence collection must begin with the most volatile and proceed to the least volatile.

System Documentation Mandate: Details of the systems from which evidence was collected—including who has access, permission levels, hardware, and software configurations—must be recorded.
📖 Toggle Exact Course Note Summary
28.1.6 RFC 3227 Order of Volatility:
Collect digital evidence in strict sequence from most volatile to least volatile:
1. Memory registers, caches
2. Routing tables, ARP cache, process table, kernel stats, RAM
3. Temporary file systems (/tmp, swap)
4. Non-volatile media (Hard disks, SSDs, USBs)
5. Remote logging and monitoring data (Syslog, SIEM, NetFlow)
6. Physical network topology and cabling
7. Archival media and backup tapes.
28.1 Evidence Handling • 28.1.7 & 28.1.8 Integrity

Chain of Custody & Data Preservation

📋

Chain of Custody Checklist

Defines collection, handling, and secure storage to prove evidence was never altered or fabricated:

  • Who discovered and collected the evidence?
  • All details of handling: times, dates, places, and personnel involved.
  • Primary responsibility: who was assigned custody and when custody transferred.
  • Physical access logs: restricted strictly to essential personnel while stored.
🛡️

Data Integrity & Preservation Rules

  • Preserve Timestamps: Avoid opening files on original media, as doing so alters access timestamps.
  • Direct Bit-Level Copies: Clone exact bit-level disk images; compare hashes of archived vs. investigated disks to prove no tampering.
  • Work on Copies Only: Analysis is conducted solely on duplicates, preserving the original disk in an untampered condition.
  • Do Not Unplug: Users should not disconnect, unplug, or power off infected machines unless explicitly directed by security staff.
📖 Course Note: Bit-level verification & Volatile RAM tools
Special tools preserve volatile RAM evidence before power-off. It should be possible to compare the archived disk image and investigated image to identify tampering. The original media must be archived and protected.

28.1 Attack Attribution • 28.1.9 & 28.1.10 MITRE ATT&CK

Attack Attribution & MITRE ATT&CK

🎯

Threat Attribution Disciplines

Threat attribution is the act of determining the individual, organization, or nation responsible for an attack.

  • Systematic Investigation: Do not allow speculation on motive to bias findings (e.g., assuming a competitor did it can blind you to nation-states).
  • TTP Correlation: Correlate Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures with threat intel (circumstantial evidence).
  • Internal Threats: Use IP addresses, MAC addresses, DHCP logs, and AAA logs (who accessed what, when).
📊

MITRE ATT&CK Framework

Global knowledge base of adversary behavior based on real-world exploits:

  • Tactics (Columns): Technical goals the attacker must accomplish (e.g., Initial Access, Persistence).
  • Techniques (Rows): Means by which tactics are accomplished.
  • Procedures: Documented real-world actions and malware implementations.
📖 Toggle Exact Course Note Summary
28.1.9 & 28.1.10 Attack Attribution & MITRE ATT&CK:
• Attribution: Identifying the threat actor responsible through IP/MAC geolocation, malware code features, and behavioral TTPs.
• MITRE ATT&CK Framework:
- Tactics: Adversary's tactical goal ('Why', e.g., Initial Access, Persistence, Exfiltration).
- Techniques: Specific means to achieve the goal ('How', e.g., Spearphishing, DLL Injection).
- Procedures: Exact implementation code or execution sequence used by a specific threat group.
28.2 The Cyber Kill Chain • Stages 1 - 3

The Cyber Kill Chain: Stages 1 to 3

Lockheed Martin Principle: If the chain is broken at any point, the attack fails, preventing damage and limiting what the adversary learns about the environment.
1. Reconnaissance STAGE 01
Adversary: Researching the target, harvesting emails, social media, public relations, network scans, and open ports.
SOC Defense: Analyzing web logs/browser analytics, monitoring scanning activity, prioritizing high-value defensive controls.
2. Weaponization STAGE 02
Adversary: Pairing an exploit targeting identified vulnerabilities with a payload/backdoor (automated weaponizers/zero-days).
SOC Defense: Keeping IDS/IPS signatures current, analyzing malware artifacts, profiling custom vs off-the-shelf weaponizer behaviors.
3. Delivery STAGE 03
Adversary: Transmitting payload via email attachments, phishing links, compromised websites, or infected USB drives.
SOC Defense: Inspecting delivery vectors/paths, filtering malicious email attachments, logging web/mail traffic for reconstruction.
📖 Toggle Exact Course Note Summary
28.2 Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain (Stages 1-3):
Developed to identify and prevent cyber intrusions. Breaking any single link stops the entire attack.
• 1. Reconnaissance: Harvest intelligence, scan ports, harvest emails, search social media. (Defense: Web/firewall log audits).
• 2. Weaponization: Pair custom backdoor with an exploit into a deliverable payload. (Defense: Threat intel, analyzing malware artifacts).
• 3. Delivery: Transmit payload via spearphishing attachment, malicious link, or USB. (Defense: Email filtering, endpoint USB controls).

28.2 The Cyber Kill Chain • Stages 4 - 7

The Cyber Kill Chain: Stages 4 to 7

4. Exploitation STAGE 04
Adversary: Triggering malicious code to exploit software, OS, or human vulnerabilities to gain execution rights.
SOC Defense: Security awareness training, regular penetration testing, endpoint hardening, vulnerability patching, secure coding.
5. Installation STAGE 05
Adversary: Establishing persistence (unauthorized services, registry Run keys, web shells) surviving reboots.
SOC Defense: Deploying HIPS, monitoring abnormal file creations, unauthorized privilege escalation, auditing endpoint activity.
6. Command & Control (CnC / C2) STAGE 06 • CRITICAL WINDOW
Adversary: Opening bi-directional channel over HTTP/HTTPS, DNS, IRC back to attacker infrastructure.
SOC Defense: Detecting beaconing, sinkholing/blocking malicious Dynamic DNS, inspecting proxy/DNS logs. Final window to block before damage!
7. Actions on Objectives STAGE 07 • IMPACT
Adversary: Credential theft, lateral movement, data exfiltration, ransom extortion, data wiping, cryptomining.
SOC Defense: Rapid incident triage, packet capture analysis, lateral movement detection, containment playbooks, damage assessment.
📖 Toggle Exact Course Note Summary
28.2 Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain (Stages 4-7):
• 4. Exploitation: Trigger malicious code against vulnerability to gain code execution. (Defense: Patching, DEP, endpoint hardening).
• 5. Installation: Establish persistent backdoor service, registry Run keys. (Defense: HIPS, file integrity monitoring).
• 6. Command & Control (C2): Open hands-on-keyboard beacon channel over HTTPS/DNS to adversary server. (Defense: DNS sinkholing, proxy inspection).
• 7. Actions on Objectives: Fulfill goal—data exfiltration, ransomware extortion, destructive wiping. (Defense: Rapid incident triage, packet capture analysis).
28.3 Diamond Model of Intrusion Analysis • 28.3.1 Overview

The Diamond Model: Core & Meta-Features

The 6 Meta-Features

Meta-features expand the model to provide crucial analytical context:

  • Timestamp: Start/stop time; integral for grouping malicious activity.
  • Phase: Steps analogous to the Cyber Kill Chain executed in succession.
  • Result: What the adversary gained (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability compromised).
  • Direction: Adversary-to-Infra, Infra-to-Victim, Victim-to-Infra, Infra-to-Adversary.
  • Methodology: Event class (e.g., port scan, phishing, content delivery attack, SYN flood).
  • Resources: Tools, adversary knowledge, funds, facilities, stolen credentials.
📖 Toggle Exact Course Note Summary
28.3.1 The Diamond Model of Intrusion Analysis:
Framework for analyzing intrusion events across 4 Core Features:
1. Adversary: Threat actor responsible for the attack.
2. Capability: Tools, malware, or exploits used.
3. Infrastructure: Physical or logical communications networks (IPs, domains, C2 servers).
4. Victim: Target organization, person, system, or email address.
6 Meta-Features: Timestamp, Phase, Result, Direction, Methodology, Resources.

28.3 Diamond Model • 28.3.2 Pivoting

Pivoting Across the Diamond Model

🔄

What is Pivoting?

The Diamond Model is ideal for illustrating how an analyst or adversary pivots from one artifact or event to the next.

Scenario: Employee reports computer acting abnormally. Scan shows malware. Malware reveals C2 domains, which resolve to IPs, exposing other infected victims and the adversary.
Each pivot correlates technical evidence with organizational scope.
Step 1: Victim discovers malware Victim ➔ Capability
Step 2: Malware contains CnC domain Capability ➔ Infrastructure
Step 3: CnC Domain resolves to IP Infrastructure
Step 4: Firewall logs reveal more victims contacting IP Infrastructure ➔ Victim
Step 5: IP ownership details reveal adversary Infrastructure ➔ Adversary
📖 Toggle Exact Course Note Summary
28.3.2 Pivoting Across the Diamond Model:
Pivoting is uncovering new information by using an established feature to discover related features.
Example: Victim discovers malware (Capability) ➔ Malware analysis reveals C2 IP (Infrastructure) ➔ Querying SIEM logs for that IP reveals second compromised host (Victim 2) ➔ Network monitoring links traffic to specific adversary group (Adversary).

28.3 Diamond Model & Kill Chain • 28.3.3 Threading

Attack Threading: Gadgets Inc. to Victim 2

🧵

Phase A: Compromise of Gadgets, Inc.

  • 1-2. Recon: Web search for Gadgets, Inc. finds gadgets.com; forum search finds network admin (NA1) email address.
  • 3-4. Delivery & Exploit: Phishing email with Trojan sent; NA1 opens malicious attachment, executing exploit.
  • 5-7. C2 & Proxy: NA1 host sends HTTP POST registering with C2; malware configured with backup C2 IPs and starts acting as a web proxy.
🎯

Phase B: Lateral Pivot to Interesting Research Inc.

  • 8. Discovery: Through NA1 proxy, adversary searches for "most important research ever", identifying Victim 2: Interesting Research Inc.
  • 9. Contact Mining: Adversary mines NA1's email contacts and discovers the Chief Research Officer (CRO).
  • 10-11. Dual Compromise: CRO receives spear-phish sent from NA1's host with the same Trojan payload. Both organizations are now compromised!
📖 DoD Diamond Model End-to-End Thread Summary
The adversary now has two compromised victims. They can mine the Chief Research Officer's email contacts for additional victims or set up another web proxy to exfiltrate proprietary research files.
28.4 Incident Response • 28.4.1 CSIRC Capability

Establishing a CSIRC: Policy, Plan, Procedures

📜

Policy Elements

Details handling based on mission, size, and function:

  • Statement of management commitment
  • Purpose, objectives, and scope
  • Definition of security incidents
  • Organizational structure & roles
  • Prioritization of severity ratings
  • Performance measures & reporting forms
🗺️

Plan Elements

Ensures clear cross-department expectations:

  • Mission, strategies, and goals
  • Senior management approval
  • Organizational approach to IR
  • Internal & external communication plans
  • Metrics measuring response capacity
  • Integration into overall organization
📋

Procedure Elements (SOPs)

Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs):

  • Technical processes & specific techniques
  • Filling out incident forms
  • Following operational checklists
  • Critical value: SOPs minimize human errors caused by personnel under extreme stress!
📖 Toggle Exact Course Note Summary
28.4.1 Establishing a CSIRC Capability:
NIST SP 800-61r2 mandates three baseline governance documents:
1. Policy: Defines mission, organizational authority, incident severity definitions, reporting mandates, and executive backing.
2. Plan: High-level roadmap detailing structure, team metrics, executive communications, and technology architecture.
3. Procedures (SOPs): Step-by-step technical standard operating procedures and forensic checklists for handling specific incident types.
28.4 Incident Response • 28.4.3 Stakeholders & CMMC

IR Stakeholders & DoD CMMC Levels

👥

Key Incident Stakeholders

  • Management: Sets policy, budgets, coordinates stakeholders, minimizes damage.
  • Information Assurance: Implements firewall rule updates during containment.
  • IT Support: Performs technical actions and assists in evidence collection.
  • Legal Department: Reviews SOPs, handles prosecution, evidence legality, lawsuits.
  • Public Affairs / PR: Manages media and public breach announcements.
  • Human Resources: Handles disciplinary action for malicious employee incidents.
  • Business Continuity & Facilities: Evaluates operational impact; secures evidence rooms.
🎖️

CMMC Incident Response Domain Levels

  • Level 2: Establish incident response plan following NIST; detect, report, prioritize, and respond via predefined procedures; root-cause analysis.
  • Level 3: Document and report incidents to designated stakeholders; regularly test IR capabilities.
  • Level 4: Use attacker TTP knowledge to refine planning; establish a 24/7 Security Operations Center (SOC).
  • Level 5: Systematic computer forensic data gathering, secure storage; deploy automated real-time responses to known incident patterns.

Cyclical Interaction

Continuous Improvement

The incident response life cycle is a self-reinforcing learning process whereby every single incident informs and improves procedures for handling future attacks.

28.4 Incident Response • 28.4.5 & 28.4.6 Vectors

Preparation, Jump Kits & Attack Vectors

🧰

Preparation: The CSIRT Jump Kit

A portable emergency kit ready for swift deployment:

  • Pre-configured laptop with forensic imaging & analysis software.
  • Sterile backup media, write-blockers, packet sniffers, network cables.
  • Critical asset lists, network topologies, baseline readings, and clean OS images.
  • Best Practice: Inspect regularly for software updates and practice deployment drills with the CSIRT.
⚡

The 6 Common Attack Vectors

  • Web: Initiated from a website or web-hosted application.
  • Email: Initiated from an email message or attachment.
  • Loss or Theft: Equipment (laptops, phones) providing attack access.
  • Impersonation: Spoofing or replacing something/someone with malicious intent.
  • Attrition: Brute force attacks against devices, networks, or services (DDoS).
  • Media: Initiated from external storage or removable media (USBs).
