Network Protocols (4.1)

Network Communications Process (4.1.1)

Views of the Network (4.1.1.1)

Operational prose:
A network can be described simultaneously as a physical substrate (copper, fiber, radio), a forwarding plane (switching/routing elements), and a control and management plane (routing protocols, controllers, management APIs). Each view emphasizes different artifacts: physical-layer impairments (attenuation, SNR, BER), forwarding-state (MAC tables, FIB/LCAM entries), and control-state (routing tables, flow entries, policy rules).

Cybersecurity Analyst View:

  • Observable artifacts: link-state changes, interface counters, CRC/frame errors, unusual link flaps, asymmetric TTLs, mismatched MTUs, and out-of-band control-plane messages.
  • Threat signals: repeated physical-layer errors may indicate cable damage or targeted jamming; rising CRCs and frame truncations can mask fragmentation-based evasion; asymmetric routing and differing ingress/egress paths appear in traceroutes and can reveal man-in-the-middle or route injection attempts.
  • Forensics: correlate NIC-level RX/TX counters, switch port counters, and system logs (dmesg, NIC driver errors) with packet captures to determine whether packet corruption is accidental or intentional.

SDN Paradigm:

  • Observable artifacts: flow tables (match/action), meter entries, counters per flow, controller-to-switch OpenFlow messages (Packet-In, Flow-Mod), and topology discovery events (LLDP).
  • Architectural consequences: control logic centralized enables global visibility but introduces new attack surface (controller compromise). Network policies are expressed as flow rules and installed reactively/proactively.
  • Operational behavior: controller decisions create succinct match-action tables; telemetry is per-flow and aggregated counters replace per-device CLI-only views. Flow churn and high reactive-installs indicate misconfiguration or a denial-of-service against the controller.
Foundational maxim: Measure the network at the interface where decisions are made — physical errors at layer 1, flow decisions at the data-plane tables, and policy at the controller.

Client-Server Communications (4.1.1.2)

Operational prose:
Client-server communication is a layered interaction pattern where clients initiate connections or requests and servers respond, often relying on discovery (DNS), transport (TCP/UDP), and application semantics (HTTP, RPC). The session typically traverses NATs, firewalls, and load balancers; each middlebox may transform headers, terminate TLS, or rewrite payloads.

Cybersecurity Analyst View:

  • Logs and traces: server access logs (HTTP status codes, bytes transferred), firewall/Audit logs (allowed/denied flows), NetFlow/IPFIX (flow durations, packet counts), and TCP state transitions visible in packet captures (SYN, SYN/ACK, ACK, FIN).
  • Anomalies: incomplete three-way handshakes indicate SYN scans or SYN flood; repeated RSTs point to middlebox interference or crafted attacks; long-lived flows with low byte rate may be C2 channels or keep-alive abuse.
  • Correlation practice: join server logs (timestamps, request URIs) with packet captures and flow records to reconstruct client-server timelines, revealing request timing, retransmission patterns, and protocol downgrade attempts.

SDN Paradigm:

  • Control-plane influence: controllers can install server-affinity flows, perform service chaining (redirect to inline inspection), and dynamically steer flows to micro-segmentation policies.
  • Programmability: client-server policies are encoded as high-level intents and compiled into flow-table rules (e.g., allow TCP dst port 443 to VIP group A); reactive Packet-In events can trigger context-aware security actions.
  • Performance/security tradeoffs: offloading TLS termination at edge proxies vs. end-to-end encryption affects visibility; SDN controllers can orchestrate TLS inspection nodes and dynamically adjust flow sampling rates for security analytics.

A Typical Session: Student (4.1.1.3)

Operational prose:
A student session typically consists of mixed web browsing, video conferencing, LMS access, and occasional file transfers. Access often uses consumer-grade Wi‑Fi or campus Ethernet; endpoints are heterogeneous (laptops, phones) and may use VPNs.

Cybersecurity Analyst View:

  • Protocol profile: frequent short-lived TLS sessions to web-based services, DTLS or WebRTC for conferencing, SFTP/HTTPS for file upload, and DNS queries for content delivery networks (CDNs).
  • Logging signatures: erratic port usage due to WebSockets or TURN; bursts of small TLS handshakes; caching behavior in HTTP headers (ETag, If-Modified-Since). Packet captures show variable round-trip times (RTT) and occasional retransmissions due to shared medium contention.
  • Attack surface: student endpoints often run unpatched clients, increasing phishing and MITM risk; look for anomalous DNS NXDOMAIN spikes, uncommon UA strings, and unexpected certificate chains.

SDN Paradigm:

  • Flow handling: controllers can tag student VLANs with specific QoS (lower priority for background sync, higher for video conferencing) and redirect suspicious flows to quarantine microservices.
  • Policy dynamics: per-user policy enforcement — dynamic rules can throttle non-academic traffic or prioritize LMS endpoints based on calendar events.
  • Observability: per-flow counters reveal per-student bandwidth use; telemetry can distinguish between interactive sessions (low jitter) and bulk transfers (high throughput, stable RTT).

Timing/Transport/security profile demands:

  • Latency sensitivity: interactive web browsing tolerates moderate latency (~50–150 ms); video conferencing needs <150 ms one-way ideally, with jitter <30 ms.
  • Transport choices: WebRTC (UDP/DTLS/SRTP) for low-latency audio/video; HTTP/2 or HTTP/3 (QUIC) for page loads — QUIC trades connection stability for lower handshake latency.
  • Windowing: browser parallelism and QUIC stream multiplexing reduce per-connection window constraints but rely on path MTU and RTT for throughput.

A Typical Session: Gamer (4.1.1.4)

Operational prose:
Gaming sessions prioritize minimal latency and low jitter. Traffic is predominantly small, frequent UDP packets for position updates, with occasional TCP/TLS for authentication, patches, and voice chat.

Cybersecurity Analyst View:

  • Traffic patterns: sustained bidirectional UDP streams with small payloads (20–200 bytes), periodic keepalive packets, and bursts during scene changes. Packet captures show tight inter-packet intervals (often <50 ms).
  • Anomaly detection: sudden packet loss, increases in RTT, or jitter spikes correlate with degraded experience; amplified outgoing traffic may indicate protocol abuse or reflection. Sequence number patterns and checksum anomalies help detect in-path modification or replay attacks.
  • Attack vectors: DDoS (both volumetric and state exhaustion via SYN/UDP floods) directly target game servers; game clients can be exploited to create botnets for reflection attacks.

SDN Paradigm:

  • Flow-programming: SDN can tag and steer gaming flows to low-latency paths, reserve bandwidth via meters, and apply QoS queueing (e.g., strict priority for UDP gaming flows).
  • Dynamic routing: controllers can implement latency-aware path selection, re-routing gaming flows around congested links in near-real-time.
  • Stateful edge functions: game-specific DPI or anti-cheat modules can be deployed inline as services and inserted via dynamic service chaining.

Timing/Transport/security profile demands:

  • Strict latency: target RTT often <50 ms for competitive play; jitter must be minimized; packet loss should be <1%.
  • Transport: UDP preferred for state updates to avoid head-of-line blocking; reliability implemented at application level (sequence numbers, delta compression).
  • Windowing: small payloads imply throughput is less important than low per-packet latency; congestion control adjustments (e.g., pacing) must avoid introducing microbursts.

A Typical Session: Surgeon (4.1.1.5)

Operational prose:
Remote surgical procedures or telesurgery require deterministic low-latency, ultra-reliable transport with strict security, often traversing dedicated fiber or high-quality MPLS circuits and employing hardware redundancy.

Cybersecurity Analyst View:

  • Observables: end-to-end latencies measured via precise timestamping (PTP/NTP synchronization), constant bandwidth streams for video and control channels, and strong cryptographic authentication logs (mutual TLS with hardware-backed keys).
  • Security posture: mandatory integrity checks, device attestation logs, and tamper-evident telemetry. Any jitter spikes or packet reordering demand immediate failover; forensic logs include signed event records for audit.
  • Threat model: high-consequence attacks (latency injection, video manipulation). Analysts monitor control-plane anomalies, certificate revocations, and device attestation failures; anomalies may trigger immediate session abort.

SDN Paradigm:

  • Network slicing: SDN enables hard-isolated slices with reserved resources (bandwidth guarantees, low-latency paths), strict admission control, and dedicated flow tables. Controllers implement deterministic QoS and path pinning.
  • Failover and redundancy: controllers orchestrate disjoint redundant paths and instant path switchover, using group table fast-failover semantics and pre-installed backup flows.
  • Security integration: integration with key-management services and hardware security modules (HSMs) orchestrated via controller policies for certificate distribution and attestation verification.

Timing/Transport/security profile demands:

  • Deterministic latency: end-to-end latency budgets often <20–50 ms; jitter budgets <10 ms; packet loss must be effectively zero for control channels.
  • Transport: hybrid approach — UDP for ultra-low latency channels with application-level forward error correction (FEC), and TLS-protected TCP or QUIC for non-real-time telemetry and logging.
  • Flow control: application-level pacing, immediate retransmit for control messages, and use of FEC to mask transient losses. Strict window sizes, minimal bufferbloat, and hardware offload for timestamping and QoS enforcement.

Tracing the Path (4.1.1.6)

Operational prose:
Tracing the packet path from source to destination requires layering: layer-2 forwarding decisions, layer-3 routing decisions, and inter-domain transit policies. Tools such as traceroute, mtr, and pathchar reveal per-hop RTTs and per-hop behavior; advanced telemetry uses in-band network telemetry (INT) or segment routing.

Cybersecurity Analyst View:

  • Artifacts: TTL decremented traces, ICMP time-exceeded messages, MPLS label stacks, and forensics from per-hop NetFlow leading to hop-by-hop packet counts and anomalies.
  • Detection: asymmetric paths, sudden hop-count changes, or unexpected intermediate ASes can indicate route hijack (BGP hijacking) or tunneling used for evasion. Packet timestamps vs. device logs permit reconstruction of latency contributions per hop.
  • Analysis technique: combine TCP timestamps and IPID changes with traceroute hops to detect in-path modification, NAT traversal, or packet duplication.

SDN Paradigm:

  • Controller visibility: SDN controllers maintain global topology and can produce exact hop lists for paths based on installed flow rules; flow-level telemetry provides per-switch counters and match metrics.
  • Programmable tracing: controller-initiated diagnostic flows can install temporary mirroring or INT headers to sample and measure latency, queue depth, and switch processing times.
  • Failure diagnosis: reactive path recomputation and path consistency checks are centrally coordinated; controllers can produce deterministic path reconstruction that includes flow-table versioning.

Communications Protocols (4.1.2)

What Are Protocols? (4.1.2.1)

Operational prose:
A protocol is a precisely defined set of rules governing the exchange of PDUs across a communication channel: syntax (format), semantics (meaning), and timing (sequence and retransmission policies). Protocol specifications enumerate header fields, state machines, allowed transitions, and error-handling rules.

Cybersecurity Analyst View:

  • Manifestations: packet headers, state transitions (e.g., TCP state machine), and logs (syslog for application-level events). Deviations from specs (invalid header lengths, mismatched checksums) are indicators of malformed traffic or evasion.
  • Detection: fingerprinting protocols via header fields, timing patterns, and handshake sequences; mismatch between declared application protocol and payload (e.g., HTTP over nonstandard ports or obfuscated payloads) is suspicious.
  • Integrity checks: checksum failures, invalid flags, and nonconforming option fields suggest tampering or exploitation attempts (e.g., buffer overflow via malformed TLV structures).

SDN Paradigm:

  • Abstraction: protocols are treated as matchable patterns in flow tables; controllers translate high-level intents into rule sets matching protocol-level headers.
  • Policy enforcement: protocol-specific policies (e.g., allow DNS only to authoritative resolvers) are centrally enforced and can be dynamically adjusted in response to detected anomalies.
  • Protocol evolution: SDN enables rapid deployment of new protocol handling by updating controller logic and pushing rules, reducing hardware upgrade cycles.

Network Protocol Suites (4.1.2.2)

Operational prose:
A protocol suite groups layered protocols that together deliver end-to-end functionality (e.g., TCP/IP suite: IP, IPv6, ICMP, TCP, UDP, application protocols like HTTP, DNS). Suites define inter-layer interfaces and expected services (reliable delivery, in-order delivery, fragmentation, addressing).

Cybersecurity Analyst View:

  • Suite-level effects: interactions produce complex artifacts: ICMP messages influence path MTU discovery; TCP and IP fragmentation affect IDS reassembly logic. Analysts must understand cross-layer interactions to avoid false positives (e.g., fragmented packets that reconstruct into an exploit).
  • Forensics: when investigating incidents, analysts inspect how different suite elements combine — e.g., how DNS-based C2 uses UDP at transport with application-layer obfuscation layered above.
  • Defense: enforce suite-level policies (e.g., block inbound unexpected ICMP types, restrict fragment handling) to reduce attack surface.

SDN Paradigm:

  • Programmatic enforcement: controllers implement suite-aware flows, e.g., match IPv4/IPv6, port numbers, and application layer signatures for deep packet actions.
  • Service chaining: SDN computes and enforces sequences of protocol-specific middleboxes (firewalls, DPI, NAT) as composable services.
  • Telemetry: suite metrics (TCP retransmit rates, UDP jitter) are exposed in controller dashboards, enabling closed-loop adjustments.

The TCP/IP Protocol Suite (4.1.2.3)

Operational prose:
TCP/IP is a de facto standard suite: Internet Protocol (IP) provides best-effort, connectionless, best-effort addressing and forwarding; TCP and UDP provide transport services (reliable byte streams vs. datagrams). IP handles fragmentation, TTL, header checksums; TCP handles reliability, flow control, congestion control; UDP is stateless and low-overhead.

Cybersecurity Analyst View:

  • TCP indicators: SYN packets show connection attempts; sequence/ack numbers and flag combinations reveal handshake progression and retransmission. Look for unusual flag combinations (e.g., SYN+FIN) as malformed/noise probes or stealth scans.
  • UDP indicators: absent connection handshakes mean detection relies on flow volume, packet signatures, or response patterns; high rate of small UDP packets may signal amplification misuse or scanning.
  • IP-level signals: TTL anomalies, protocol numbers, and fragmentation behavior are key for detecting tunneling and evasion; IPv6 introduces new extension headers that need special attention.

SDN Paradigm:

  • Flow rule granularity: match on IP protocol, ports, DSCP, and even TCP flags to enact fine-grained controls. SDN enables targeted rate-limiting (meters) and per-flow statistics for TCP/UDP balancing.
  • Congestion handling: controllers can proactively reroute heavy TCP flows, adjust priority queues, or apply shaping at ingress to maintain application SLAs.
  • Middlebox orchestration: NAT, firewall, and TCP proxies can be programmatically inserted or removed, addressing TCP/IP suite interactions such as connection tracking consistency.

Format, Size, and Timing (4.1.2.4)

Format

Operational prose:
Format defines the syntax and structure of headers and payloads: fixed-length fields, variable-length TLVs, alignment, endianness, and option encoding. Precise format rules enable deterministic parsing and validation.

Cybersecurity Analyst View:

  • Indicators: incorrect header lengths, unusual option values, or nonstandard padding are common signs of crafted packets; IDS heuristics flag deviations from RFC-defined formats.
  • Exploits: format anomalies are exploited (e.g., malformed TLV fields to trigger parser bugs). Capture-based analysis must include reassembly and normalization to detect such payloads.
  • Forensics: preserve raw captures with timestamps; reparse with multiple decoders (libpcap, custom parsers) to detect discrepancies in interpretation.
Size

Operational prose:
Size constraints operate across layers: MTU determines largest frame payload; MSS (TCP) determines segment payload taking headers and options into account; application payload sizes influence fragmentation and segmentation.

Cybersecurity Analyst View:

  • Artifacts: fragmentation patterns (overlapping fragments, excessive fragmentation) are used to evade signature-based detection (fragmentation overlap attacks). Monitor DF bit behavior and Path MTU Discovery failures.
  • Indicators: many small packets (small MSS) can indicate either application behavior (interactive) or attempts to exhaust per-flow state (state-exhaustion attacks).
  • Investigation: reconstruct reassembled PDUs; verify whether fragmentation conforms to standards and whether fragments are maliciously overlapped or reordered.
Timing

Operational prose:
Timing encompasses inter-packet intervals, retransmission timers, RTT measurements, and protocol timers (e.g., TCP RTO, TCB timeouts, ARP cache expiry). Timing controls reliability and performance and is often exploited by attackers.

Cybersecurity Analyst View:

  • Observables: RTT time series, retransmit counts, delayed ACK patterns, and flow durations. Sudden RTO spikes indicate congestion or path change.
  • Attack detection: timing side-channel attacks, slowloris (slow HTTP requests), or deliberate jitter injection to disrupt real-time apps. Time-correlated anomalies across hosts often indicate systemic issues.
  • Parameter specifics: TCP RTO calculation uses Smoothed RTT (SRTT) and RTT variance (RTTVAR); miscalculated RTOs produce spurious retransmits or stalls.
Foundational maxim: Timing is a protocol's second state machine—format and size define the unit, timing defines the rhythm.

Unicast, Multicast, and Broadcast (4.1.2.5)

Operational prose:
These are delivery semantics. Unicast targets a single destination, multicast targets group addresses with optimized replication, and broadcast targets all nodes on a broadcast domain. Each behaves differently at L2/L3 and when traversing routers or SDN controllers.

Cybersecurity Analyst View:

  • Unicast: typical flow logs, per-destination flow accounting. Anomalous unicast patterns: scanning (many dest IPs, single source) or exfiltration (sustained upload to single external IP).
  • Multicast: requires IGMP/MLD and multicast routing (PIM). Analysts inspect IGMP join/leave messages; unexpected joins or unauthorized multicast sources may be abused for reflection or traffic amplification.
  • Broadcast: ARP scams, broadcast storms manifest as high broadcast packet rates causing saturated link counters. Broadcast-based attacks can be used for LAN enumeration or amplification.

SDN Paradigm:

  • Unicast: controllers program exact match rules for per-host flows; can implement fine-grained access control lists.
  • Multicast: SDN simplifies multicast by installing group table entries for replication or by using controller-based multicast trees; dynamic group membership is handled via controller orchestration.
  • Broadcast: Controllers can snoop and suppress broadcast floods by installing broadcast-limiting rules or by converting broadcast semantics to controller-mediated replication (reducing storm risk).

Reference Models (4.1.2.6)

The OSI Reference Model

Operational prose:
OSI defines seven conceptual layers (Physical, Data Link, Network, Transport, Session, Presentation, Application). It formalizes interfaces and PDU lifecycle but is a modelling tool rather than a prescriptive implementation in many networks.

Cybersecurity Analyst View:

  • Layered logs: each layer creates artifacts: PHY (interface counters), L2 (MAC tables), L3 (routing logs), L4 (TCP status), L7 (application logs).
  • Threat mapping: map attacks to layers—e.g., MAC spoofing (L2), IP spoofing/BGP hijack (L3), SYN flood (L4), SQL injection (L7). Security controls should be applied at multiple layers to provide defense in depth.
  • Reassembly and normalization: IDS must reassemble PDUs across multiple layers (IP fragments → TCP segments → application messages) to detect layered exploits.
SDN Paradigm:

Practical mapping: SDN collapses several OSI concerns into programmable flows; control plane corresponds to OSI management layers; data plane corresponds to L2/L3 forwarding. Controllers may present abstracted “northbound” APIs representing combined layer functionality.

The TCP/IP Protocol Model

Operational prose:
TCP/IP uses fewer layers: Link (Network Interface), Internet (IP), Transport (TCP/UDP), Application (HTTP/DNS/etc.). The model maps directly to deployed stacks and is operationally predominant.

Cybersecurity Analyst View:

  • Pragmatics: analysts work primarily with TCP/IP due to real-world traffic; map OSI conceptual layers into TCP/IP when investigating.
  • Forensics: packet captures are interpreted according to this model; TCP state machine analysis and IP-layer routing behavior are core.

SDN Paradigm:

  • Controller design: SDN’s abstractions align with TCP/IP layers—flow rules target L2/L3/L4 headers; application-layer considerations are handled at higher-level controllers or application-specific proxies.

Three Addresses (4.1.2.7)

Operational prose:
Networking uses multiple addressing scopes: physical (MAC) for L2 local delivery, network (IP) for end-to-end routing, and application (port, URI, user ID) for service demultiplexing and identity. Proper mapping between them (ARP, NDP, NAT) is essential.

Cybersecurity Analyst View:

  • Observable mappings: ARP cache contents, NAT translations (ip:port <-> external ip:port), and socket tables (local:port -> PID). Correlate logs across these mappings to attribute flows to processes and users.
  • Exploits: ARP spoofing changes L2->L3 mapping; NAT logs provide necessary attribution in NATted environments. Missing or inconsistent mappings (e.g., IP seen but no ARP entry) can indicate spoofing or tunneling.
  • Forensics practice: track tuple (src MAC, src IP, src port) over time; use switch port to MAC mappings to localize endpoints.

SDN Paradigm:

  • Controller-managed addressing: SDN can maintain host-to-location maps, reducing reliance on broadcast ARP storms by answering ARP via controller and populating flow tables.
  • NAT and virtualization: controllers orchestrate overlay addressing (VXLAN/NVGRE) and maintain mapping between underlay IPs and overlay VTEP addresses.

PDU lifecycle mapping:
Bits → Frames (L2: MAC src/dst, Ethertype) → Packets (L3: IP src/dst, TTL, protocol) → Segments (L4: TCP/UDP ports, seq/ack) → Data (L7 application payload).

Encapsulation (4.1.2.8)

Operational prose:
Encapsulation wraps higher-layer PDUs inside lower-layer frames: application data inside TCP segments inside IP packets inside Ethernet frames. Each encapsulation adds headers/trailers and possibly transforms or fragments payloads.

Cybersecurity Analyst View:

  • Detection: track headers added/removed by NAT, VPN, or tunneling (GRE, IPsec, VXLAN). Encapsulation can hide inner protocol characteristics; nested encapsulation complicates IDS signature matching.
  • Abuse: tunneling protocols are used to evade controls (e.g., HTTP tunneling, DNS tunneling); packet inspection must be able to decapsulate and inspect inner payloads to detect covert channels.
  • Forensics: preserve original encapsulation order in captures; examine outer headers for routing artifacts and inner headers for malicious content.

SDN Paradigm:

  • Overlay/underlay: SDN manages overlays (e.g., VXLAN) by programming VTEP behavior; controller maintains mapping of virtual to physical endpoints.
  • Match-action and encapsulation: programmable match on outer encapsulation fields or perform de-encapsulation at edge devices; controllers can selectively de-encapsulate and apply L7 policies.

Detailed PDU lifecycle example:

  1. Application layer: HTTP GET (Data)
  2. Transport layer: TCP segment with src/dst ports, seq/ack, window
  3. Network layer: IP packet with src/dst IP, TTL, fragmentation flags
  4. Data link layer: Ethernet frame with src/dst MAC, VLAN tag
  5. Physical: bits transmitted over medium

Scenario: Sending and Receiving a Web Page (4.1.2.9)

Operational prose (end-to-end sequence with PDUs and timers):

  1. DNS resolution: client issues UDP DNS query (or DNS over TLS/HTTPS) to resolver. PDU: UDP datagram encapsulated in IP packet → Ethernet frame → bits. Timers: DNS client RTO ≈ 1–2 s, with retries; DNS over TLS includes TLS handshake RTTs.
  2. TCP three-way handshake (if HTTP over TCP) or QUIC handshake (if HTTP/3 over UDP): TCP: Client sends SYN (seq=x) — server replies SYN/ACK (seq=y, ack=x+1) — client sends ACK (ack=y+1). RTO and backoff apply; RTO computed from SRTT and RTTVAR (RFC 6298). QUIC: 0-RTT/1-RTT handshake reduces latency but possible replay risks; handshake includes TLS 1.3 handshake integrated with transport.
  3. TLS handshake (if HTTPS): exchange of ClientHello, ServerHello, certificate, key exchange, finished messages. TLS Record Layers carry handshake messages; session establishment includes key derivation (HKDF), cipher suite negotiation, and potential client and server authentication. Observables: SNI in ClientHello (unless encrypted), JA3 fingerprint, certificate chain. Analysts see TLS handshake flow lengths and ciphers used.
  4. HTTP request/response: Client sends GET; server responds with headers and content (often chunked). For large content, TCP segments and possibly application-level chunking and compression (gzip) come into play.
  5. Connection teardown and persistence: HTTP/1.1 persistent connections use keep-alive; HTTP/2 multiplexes streams over single connection; HTTP/3 uses QUIC streams over UDP.
  6. Caching and CDN interactions: Client may receive cached responses; requests may be redirected to CDN or load balancers.

Cybersecurity Analyst View (detection, anomalies, and logs):

  • DNS stage: unusual resolver IPs or DNS over nonstandard ports — look for NXDOMAIN floods and sudden increases in TXT record queries (tunneling).
  • Handshake stage: mismatched TLS versions, deprecated cipher suites, or suspicious JA3 fingerprints indicate nonstandard clients or malware. Certificate anomalies (short-lived certs, unusual issuers) are red flags.
  • Transport phase: frequent retransmissions, packet reordering, or inconsistent MSS across connections indicate path MTU or middlebox issues. RST storms or half-open connections point to SYN flood or middlebox resets.
  • Application phase: unusual request URIs, header anomalies, or unexpected content-encoding indicate exploitation attempts. HTTP/2 or HTTP/3 unusual stream patterns (stream resets) can point to abused multiplexing.
  • Forensics: correlate server access logs (timestamps, URIs) with packet captures and TLS logs; extract session keys where possible (session tickets or TLS key logs) for deeper analysis.

SDN Paradigm (controller actions, telemetry, and enforcement):

  • Controller pre-provisioning: for known web services, controller installs proactive flow rules for VIPs, ARP/ND snooping, and route selection to CDN egress points.
  • Reactive control: first packet of new flow triggers Packet-In; controller authorizes and pushes a Flow-Mod to install match-action rules that handle subsequent packets at wire speed.
  • Service chaining and inspection: controller may route the flow through an inline TLS proxy for inspection then to the appropriate backend pool; group table entries enable load balancing across server instances.
  • Telemetry & adaptation: controller collects per-flow counters (packets/bytes, duration) and RTT estimates; anomalous handshake latencies or repeated packet-in events trigger adaptive policies (e.g., increase sampling, divert to scrubbing service).
  • Failure handling: if middlebox or server responds with unexpected RSTs, controller can reroute flows, update flow-table entries, or temporarily blacklist a problematic backend.

Detailed timer and window specifics involved:

  • DNS: client RTO ~1s, exponential backoff; resolver limits UDP query rates.
  • TCP handshake: SYN retransmit timers start at RTO; typical initial RTO ~1s (OS-dependent); client cwnd initial (IW) typically 10 segments (RFC 6928) for modern stacks; advertised window and SACK options influence recovery.
  • TCP congestion control: SRTT influences cwnd growth; slow start to congestion avoidance with Reno/Cubic algorithms; fast retransmit triggered by 3 duplicate ACKs; fast recovery algorithms manage cwnd reduction.
  • TLS: handshake includes retransmission timers; TLS record retransmits are handled by underlying transport; TLS 1.3 truncates handshake steps reducing RTTs.
  • QUIC: integrates congestion and loss recovery with faster connections and 0-RTT possibilities; connection IDs enable mobility through NATs.

Attack and anomaly scenarios and how they appear:

  • SYN flood: high SYN rate with low ACKs; server SYN queue fill-up; NetFlow shows high half-open flows; SDN reacts by rate-limiting SYNs or offloading to SYN proxies.
  • Fragmentation overlap: packet fragments with overlapping offsets used to bypass IDS; reassembly logs inconsistent or show payloads that match exploit signatures only after normalization.
  • TLS downgrade: client/server advertise lower ciphers; logs show deprecated ciphers negotiated; JA3/JA3S mismatch indicates TLS manipulation.
  • DNS tunneling: elevated TXT or long subdomain queries; flow logs reveal many small DNS requests with data-like payloads; SDN can enforce DNS egress filtering.

Step-by-step PDU example (single HTTP GET over HTTPS):

  1. L7: "GET /index.html HTTP/1.1\r\nHost: example.com\r\n..."
  2. L4: TCP segment srcPort=54321 dstPort=443, seq=1000, ack=0, flags=SYN, window=65535, options=MSS=1460,SACKOK,TS
  3. L3: IP packet src=10.0.0.5 dst=93.184.216.34, TTL=64, protocol=6
  4. L2: Ethernet frame srcMAC=00:11:22:33:44:55 dstMAC=66:77:88:99:AA:BB ethertype=0x0800
  5. Physical: bits transmitted over fiber/copper/wireless medium

Forensic reconstruction:

  • Align timestamps from client logs, switch port ingress timestamps, and server logs to compute per-hop delays and to localize whether delays originate at access, last-mile, or server processing.
  • Use TCP sequence and ACK numbers, along with SRTT evolution, to reconstruct retransmits and congestion behavior.
